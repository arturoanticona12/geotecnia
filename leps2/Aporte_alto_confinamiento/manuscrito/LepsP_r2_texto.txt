4 Abstract
5 The Leps 2.0 envelopes, calibrated on a public database of more than 900 large-scale triaxial 
6 tests, are the standard empirical estimate of the drained shear strength of rockfill and mine waste 
7 rock. Reliability-based design of waste dumps up to 700 m high also needs the uncertainty of 
8 that estimate, its dependence on stress, and a rule to combine it with site tests. Leps-P is a three￾9 level linear mixed model of the Leps power envelope in which the reference secant friction angle 
10 and the curvature vary between publications and between materials, the within-material 
11 dispersion depends on stress, and variance parameters are sampled by Markov chain Monte 
12 Carlo. It was calibrated on 767 tests from 36 publications and evaluated by leaving out one 
13 publication at a time. Without site tests, the 80% predictive interval covered 83% of held-out 
14 tests, with an accuracy equal to that of the Leps 2.0 envelope with constant dispersion. With a 
15 few site tests at low stress, updating only the level of the envelope reduced the continuous ranked 
16 probability score at higher stress from 0.041 to 0.030; updating the curvature as well was 
17 overconfident, and a least-squares fit with the curvature fixed at 0.86 performed similarly. 
18 Between-publication differences accounted for 38% of the between-material variance of the 
19 reference angle. Within-material comparisons showed a heterogeneous specimen-size effect, 
20 negative only for nearly parallel gradations. For waste rock from a polymetallic skarn deposit, 
21 the placement void ratio, untested lithologies, and the pore pressure generated in a saturated 
22 basal layer by rapid loading controlled the factor of safety of a 700 m dump more than the

23 extrapolation of the envelope beyond the data. The model, code, and parameters can be re￾24 estimated as tests at higher stress become available.
25 Practical Applications
26 Designers of high waste dumps and rockfill dams often have few or no large-scale shear tests of 
27 their own and rely on published charts of rockfill strength. This work turns the most widely used 
28 chart into a tool that gives a best estimate of strength together with its range, shows how that 
29 range grows at the high stresses found under tall dumps, and shows how much it narrows when a 
30 few laboratory tests of the site material are added. The tool also warns the user when site tests 
31 disagree with published experience. An example for a very high dump shows that the safety 
32 margin depends strongly on how loosely the waste rock is placed, on rock types that have not 
33 been tested, and on keeping the base of the dump drained during construction. Fast advance of 
34 the dump over a saturated base of low permeability can cause failure even when the strength of 
35 the waste rock is well known. Testing every major rock type at large scale, measuring the density 
36 of the placed material, and controlling the rate of construction over saturated ground are the most 
37 effective actions. Free software accompanies the paper.

40 Introduction
41 Leps (1970) compiled 109 large-scale triaxial tests and proposed three nonlinear 
42 envelopes for rockfill. For five decades, these curves have been used for dams and waste dumps 
43 whenever site-specific tests were unavailable. Cylwik et al. (2025) updated them with a public 
44 database of 938 tests (Arrieta 2025), adopted the power criterion of De Mello (1977) and Charles

and Watts (1980) with a single fitting parameter, the secant friction angle at 𝜎𝑛
′ 45 = 10𝑝𝑎, and 
46 related that angle to rock quality and void ratio. That update is referred to as Leps 2.0.
47 Two developments in mining practice motivate a further extension. First, waste dumps 
48 now reach 500–700 m (Valenzuela et al. 2008), so effective normal stresses at their base can 
49 exceed the 7.9 MPa reached in the database, in a material that crushes, loses dilatancy and 
50 permeability, and may respond contractively (Linero et al. 2007; Ovalle et al. 2020); loose, 
51 saturated waste on weak foundations has produced rapid flow slides (Hungr et al. 2002). Second, 
52 design is moving toward reliability-based criteria (Read and Stacey 2009; Hawley and Cunning 
53 2017), which require a strength distribution and a rule to combine published experience with site 
54 tests.
55 Hierarchical models that borrow strength from generic databases have been developed for 
56 soil properties (Ching and Phoon 2019; Ching et al. 2021), and a hierarchical Bayesian analysis 
57 of the Barton and Kjærnsli (1981) parameters has been applied to rockfill groups of a single 
58 project (Contreras et al. 2022; Contreras and Linero-Molina 2025). The novelty of this work lies 
59 not in the hierarchical framework itself but in (1) its calibration on the Leps 2.0 database with 
60 random reference angle and curvature; (2) the separation of publication and material effects; (3) 
61 out-of-sample evaluation by publication, including a comparison with the simple fits used in 
62 practice; (4) a validated site-updating rule with a prior–data conflict check; and (5) explicit 
63 treatment of specimen size, placement density, and pore pressure in the design of high dumps. 
64 Leps-P is an independent extension built on the public Leps 2.0 database; it is not affiliated with 
65 the authors of Leps 2.0.

126 Metropolis algorithm under weakly informative priors (half-normal with scale 0.25 on standard

129 al. 2013). A quadratic form (Eq. 2) and a power form (𝑐2 = 0) were fitted.

149 Mode A: No Site Tests
150 Leaving out each publication, the 80% interval covered 83% of the tests (95% confidence 
151 interval 78%–88%) and 9% fell below the 10th percentile (Fig. 3, Table 2). The CRPS of Leps-P 
152 and of the reference did not differ significantly (difference +0.0008, 95% CI −0.0048 to 
153 +0.0075): without site tests, Leps-P does not predict the strength of a new material better than 
154 Leps 2.0 with a constant dispersion, but it provides a stress-dependent description of uncertainty. 
155 Above 2.75 MPa the intervals were conservative (88% coverage, 4% below the 10th percentile, 
156 95% CI 0%–13%), mainly because the predicted median was low for some publications, and the

183 Specimen-Size Effect
184 Nineteen materials were tested at more than one maximum particle size (Fig. 5). Across 
185 all of them, the random-effects mean of 𝛽𝐷 was −0.004 (Hartung–Knapp 95% CI −0.074 to 
186 +0.066; Hartung and Knapp 2001; DerSimonian and Laird 1986) with a between-material 
187 standard deviation of 0.063. For 8 materials with nearly parallel gradation and similar density, 
188 𝛽𝐷 was −0.040 (95% CI −0.065 to −0.016), equivalent to κ = 0.25 (0.10–0.40), consistent in sign 
189 and compatible in magnitude with Weibull scaling. This result depends on the similarity 
190 criterion: stricter or looser criteria gave intervals that include zero. In a meta-regression, an 
191 increase of the uniformity coefficient with size tended to raise 𝛽𝐷 (coefficient +0.072 ± 0.048), 
192 while particle angularity had no clear effect, contrary to the explanation of Varadarajan et al. 
193 (2003). Size therefore enters Leps-P as an uncertainty: by default with the distribution from all 
194 groups, and, when field and laboratory gradations are parallel, with the parallel-grading 
195 distribution.

205 Envelopes by Lithology and Prior–Data Conflict
206 The conflict check flagged 3 of the 5 sets (Fig. 6a): both hornfels sets (p < 0.001), whose 
207 secant angle increased with stress while the specimens were still contracting at the maximum 
208 deviator, and one intrusive set (p = 0.038). Increasing secant angles over this range are unusual; 
209 densification during consolidation, which is collinear with confining stress in these sets, is a 
210 plausible cause that the data cannot isolate. The flagged sets were not used to update the 
211 envelope; for hornfels the prior was kept, which is conservative here because the tests are 
212 stronger than the prior. The remaining sets were updated jointly with a common laboratory 
213 effect. The slope restriction retained 92% of the joint samples. When the marble was assigned to 
214 the good class instead, its set was flagged (p = 0.042) because the tests are weaker than that 
215 prior, so the conflict check also discriminates between candidate classes. Keeping the good-class 
prior for the marble would have raised the median and 5th percentile of the dump-average 𝜙𝑠

217 placed waste rock at 3 MPa by 2.1° and 1.6°; the low class, which agrees with the tests, was

263 Discussion
264 What Leps-P Adds
265 Leps-P does not predict the central strength of an untested material better than Leps 2.0, 
266 and with a few site tests it performs similarly to a careful least-squares fit with the curvature 
267 fixed at 0.86. Its contributions are the population curvature itself, which corrects the bias of fits 
268 with m = 0.9; a calibrated, stress-dependent uncertainty without site tests; a validated updating 
269 rule with a conflict check; the separation of publication and material variability; and a framework 
270 in which placement density, specimen size, correlation between lithologies, and pore pressure 
271 enter a single reliability analysis.

272 Design Format
273 A design procedure consistent with these results is: (1) assign rock quality and placement 
274 void ratio to each lithology and check the conflict diagnostic for any site tests; (2) update with 
275 tests that pass, at the level only; (3) shift the envelopes to field particle size and placement 
276 density; (4) adopt as characteristic value for a global mechanism the 5th percentile of the mean 
277 envelope, a cautious estimate of the mean in the sense of Eurocode 7 (CEN 2004; Prästings et al. 
278 2019); (5) check the probability of failure by sampling the envelopes with stated correlations 
279 between lithologies, including the weakest-lithology and pore-pressure scenarios; and (6) 
280 calibrate the target factor of safety for the 5th-percentile envelope to the consequence class rather 
281 than applying factors calibrated for best-estimate parameters (Read and Stacey 2009), because 
282 the strength uncertainty would otherwise be counted twice.

294 Conclusions
295 1. Without site tests, the Leps-P 80% interval covered 83% of tests from held-out publications, 
296 with accuracy equal to that of Leps 2.0 with constant dispersion and conservative intervals 
297 above 2.75 MPa.
298 2. With a few site tests at low stress, updating only the level of the envelope reduced the CRPS 
299 at higher stress from 0.041 to 0.030; updating the curvature was overconfident, and a least￾300 squares fit with m fixed at 0.86 performed similarly.
301 3. Between-publication differences accounted for 38% of the between-set variance of the 
302 reference angle and most of that of the curvature.
303 4. The specimen-size effect was heterogeneous; for nearly parallel gradations it was negative 
304 and compatible with Weibull scaling.
305 5. For a 700 m dump of skarn waste rock, placement density, untested lithologies, and pore 
306 pressure in a saturated basal layer under rapid loading controlled the factor of safety more 
307 than the extrapolation of the envelope.
