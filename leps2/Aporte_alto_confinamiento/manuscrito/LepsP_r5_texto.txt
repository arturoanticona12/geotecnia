[0] Leps-P: Probabilistic Strength Envelopes with Stress-Decaying Curvature for Rockfill and Mine Waste Rock

[1] Author names, ASCE grade, affiliations, and corresponding author e-mail to be completed.

[2] Abstract

[3] Reliability-based design of high waste dumps needs the drained shear strength of rockfill and mine waste rock as a distribution and a rule to combine published experience with site tests. Leps-P is a three-level hierarchical model of the secant friction angle in which the dilatant part of strength decays with stress toward an asymptote, with a material-level dilatant level and decay rate, calibrated on 767 large-scale triaxial tests from 36 publications of the Leps 2.0 database. In maximum likelihood, this form exceeded a quadratic log–log envelope with the same number of parameters by 9.7 and a form without an asymptote by 12.2; the asymptote, about 24°, is a decay parameter, not a critical-state angle. Leaving out one publication at a time, the bounded, quadratic, and Leps 2.0 envelopes predicted new materials with indistinguishable accuracy; the hierarchical forms had intervals consistent with nominal coverage at low stress, unlike a constant dispersion, and underpredicted the median above 2.75 MPa. Updating the level of each material with a few site tests at low stress gave nominal coverage at higher stress (83% of the 80% interval). Under the adopted size model, 52–62% of the variance for an untested waste rock at field scale came from the heterogeneity of the specimen-size effect. For a 700 m dump on a competent foundation, whose critical surface mobilized  ≤ 2.0 MPa, the Morgenstern–Price factor of safety was 1.47 with the median and 1.21 with the 5th-percentile envelope of placed waste rock. Design charts and open code are provided.

[4] Practical Applications

[5] Designers of high waste dumps and rockfill dams often have few or no large-scale shear tests and rely on published charts of rockfill strength. This work turns the Leps chart into a tool that gives a best estimate of strength and a cautious value for design, for any rock quality and density, together with the range of uncertainty. Its curves flatten at high pressure, as expected when crushed rock stops dilating, and they are supported by tests up to about 8 MPa on the failure plane. A few tests of the site material at low pressure sharpen the estimate at higher pressure, and the tool warns when site tests disagree with published experience. For untested waste rock, most of the remaining uncertainty comes, under the adopted model, from the size of the rock particles and from differences between materials and laboratories; testing the actual waste rock at large scale and at two particle sizes is therefore the most valuable investigation. Free software accompanies the paper.

[7] Introduction

[8] Leps (1970) compiled 109 large-scale triaxial tests and proposed three nonlinear envelopes for rockfill that have been used for dams and waste dumps whenever site-specific tests were unavailable. Cylwik et al. (2025) updated them with a public database of 938 tests (Arrieta 2025), adopted the power criterion of De Mello (1977) and Charles and Watts (1980) with a single fitting parameter, the secant friction angle at  = 10, and related that angle to rock quality and void ratio. That update is referred to here as Leps 2.0.

[9] Two developments motivate a further extension. First, design is moving toward reliability-based criteria (Read and Stacey 2009; Hawley and Cunning 2017), which require a strength distribution and a rule to combine published experience with site tests. Second, waste dumps now reach 500–700 m (Valenzuela et al. 2008). For a uniform dump on a competent foundation the critical surface stays at normal stresses of about 2 MPa (see the application below), but mechanisms controlled by the foundation, by a basal layer, or by the weakest material reach higher stresses, where waste rock crushes and loses dilatancy (Marsal 1973; Hardin 1985; Lade et al. 1996; Linero et al. 2007; Ovalle et al. 2020). How an envelope behaves at the edge of and beyond the data then matters: a power law with a constant exponent keeps curving downward, whereas the excess of strength over the critical state, which is the contribution of dilatancy, is suppressed by confinement (Bolton 1986; Jefferies and Been 2016).

[10] Hierarchical models that borrow strength from generic databases have been developed for soil properties (Ching and Phoon 2019; Ching et al. 2021), and a hierarchical Bayesian analysis of the Barton and Kjærnsli (1981) parameters has been applied to the rockfill groups of a single project (Contreras et al. 2022; Contreras and Linero-Molina 2025). Leps-P differs in pooling 36 publications, in its envelope form, and in its out-of-sample evaluation. Its contributions are a material-level envelope whose curvature decays with stress, a site-updating rule with a prior–data conflict check, a decomposition of the uncertainty of an untested waste rock at field scale, and design charts with an open implementation.

[11] Database and Model Formulation

[12] Calibration Database

[13] The public database (Arrieta 2025) was filtered to tests with  ≥ 60 kPa, reported initial void ratio, and at least two tests per set, giving 767 tests on 226 sets from 36 publications (Fig. S1). A set is a material with a given gradation, density, and rock type tested in one program; failure is the maximum deviator stress, as in Leps 2.0. Rock quality classes follow Table 5 of Cylwik et al. (2025). Tests with  > 3 MPa (90 tests) come from eight publications, seven of them published between 1963 and 1983 (e.g., Marachi et al. 1972; Marsal 1973), and sets with  < 31° were excluded by Cylwik et al. (2025), so the lower tail of the population is truncated. The net volumetric strain at the maximum deviator, reported for 308 tests, showed compression that grew with stress: its median was -0.9% below 0.5 MPa and -6.9% above 3 MPa, where the median breakage index of Marsal (1973) was 36% (Fig. S2); the dilatancy rate at the peak is not reported. Above 5 MPa the measured secant angles ranged from 35.5° to 41.2° (5th to 95th percentile, 34 tests). The database void ratio ranges from 0.15 to 0.89, and 83 of the 90 tests above 3 MPa have e ≤ 0.55.

[41] Estimation and Out-of-Sample Evaluation

[42] Fixed effects were estimated by Gauss–Newton iteration on the linearized mean, with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) for the variance parameters. Variance parameters were sampled with an adaptive Metropolis algorithm under weakly informative priors (half-normal with scale 0.25 on standard deviations, uniform on correlations, normal on ), with four chains from dispersed starting values; the fixed effects are thus estimated conditionally (an empirical Bayes scheme). Convergence was checked with the rank-normalized potential scale reduction factor and bulk and tail effective sample sizes (Vehtari et al. 2021). Comparisons between mean structures used the full maximum likelihood, with the variance parameters refitted for each structure.

[43] Each publication was left out in turn; in each fold (, ) were selected on the training data only ( from 16° to 30° in steps of 2° and  from 0.20 to 0.45 in steps of 0.05, at the variance parameters of a common starting fit) and the model was refitted. In Mode A (no site tests), every test of the held-out publication was predicted. In Mode B, each held-out set with at least two tests at  ≤ 1.5 MPa and one at  ≥ 2 MPa was updated with the former, and the latter were predicted with the prior, the level update of each set (Eq. 8), variants with discarded or jointly updated sets, and least-squares fits of  with m = 0.90 or with m from the training data (Table S1). These procedures were developed on the same held-out sets, so the Mode B comparisons are exploratory. Scores were the coverage of the 80% interval, the fraction of tests below the 10th percentile, the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS) of ln τ (Gneiting and Raftery 2007), and, for the 10th percentile, the quantile (pinball) loss. Differences in publication means were tested with exact sign-flip tests with Holm (1979) adjustment within each mode; bootstrap intervals over publications are given for description only, since they are anti-conservative with 9–13 publications. The Mode A reference was the Leps 2.0 envelope (m = 0.9) with a constant dispersion fitted to the training data; its constants were fitted to the whole database, which favors it.

[44] The folds used plug-in predictive distributions. With all data, the distribution of the implementation differed from the plug-in one by at most 0.004 in median ln τ and had a standard deviation 1.03–1.09 times as large. The single-material update of the implementation is algebraically the Mode B level update; the joint site update applies the same rule with a shared laboratory effect.

[49] Mode A: No Site Tests

[50] Across all held-out tests (Table 2, Fig. 2a), the bounded and quadratic forms had CRPS 0.0680 and 0.0688 (−0.0008 (95% CI −0.0036 to +0.0015; sign-flip p = 0.56, Holm-adjusted 1.00)), and Leps-P covered 84% of tests with its 80% interval. [51] Mode B: Site Tests at Low Stress

[52] With the level update of each set, 83% of the held-out tests fell within the 80% interval (binomial 95% interval about 71–91%, before allowing for clustering) and the standard deviation of standardized residuals was 0.91, consistent with nominal (Table 2, Fig. 2b). The CRPS was 0.0308, against 0.0424 for the prior, 0.0369 for the least-squares fit with m from the training data, and 0.0407 for the fit with m = 0.90, which placed 17% of tests below its 10th percentile. The joint update scored 0.0330 with heavy tails (standard deviation of standardized residuals 1.34). Discarding flagged sets did not improve the scores (0.0345 alone and 0.0344 with a shared laboratory effect), so the evaluation does not support exclusion as a general rule. None of the differences was significant after adjustment (smallest Holm-adjusted p = 0.48; all procedures in Table S1); with 64 tests from 13 publications the comparison can detect only large differences. The conflict check flagged 9% of the sets.

[59] Application: Waste Rock from a Large Open-Pit Mine

[60] Site Data and Conflict Check

[61] The waste rock of a large open-pit mine comprises five rock types, A to E. Drained triaxial tests on large specimens of scalped gradation by one laboratory were available for A, B, and C (5 sets); D and E were untested, and E is prone to degradation. Rock quality was taken as low for A, C, D, and E and moderate for B; illustrative mass fractions of 35%, 25%, 20%, 10%, and 10% were used. For rock type A the conflict check gave p = 0.17 with the low class and 0.025 with the good class, so the tests were consistent with the class assigned. The check flagged 3 of the 5 sets (Fig. 3a): both sets of B (p ≤ 0.0001) and one of C (p = 0.022). In the B sets the secant angle increased with stress and the specimens were still compressing at the maximum deviator, reached at 13–19% axial strain. With the deviator at 10% and 15% axial strain their free log–log slopes were still 1.04–1.19, so the increase is not an artifact of the failure definition, and fixed-strain criteria created new conflicts in the other rock types. Because the flagged sets are stronger than the prior at high stress, they were not used to update the envelope, which is conservative here; the remaining sets were updated jointly with a common laboratory effect. Each rock type is represented by one material, so the updated envelope describes the tested sample of that rock type rather than its variability within the dump.

[62] Placement Density, Specimen Size, and Dump-Average Envelope

[63] Laboratory specimens had void ratios of 0.31–0.42; a placement void ratio of 0.65 was adopted for end-dumped waste rock, with an additional increase of 0.15 for the degradation of E. A failure surface through a mixed dump mobilizes the mass-weighted strength of the rock types it crosses; this assumes mixing along the surface, and the weakest-rock-type case bounds segregated placement. At  = 1 MPa, typical of the critical surfaces below, the median and 5th percentile of the dump-average  were 41.5° and 39.7° at laboratory density and size, 36.4° and 34.6° at placement density without size shift, and 36.4° and 31.1° with both (Table 4; Fig. 3b). Density thus lowered the median and size uncertainty the 5th percentile. The size shift was shared by all rock types; with independent shifts the 5th percentile of the dump average was 33.5°, so sharing it is conservative. A surface confined to E lowered the 5th percentile by 3.7°, and giving D and E a laboratory effect independent of the site changed it by 0.4°. Only 23 database tests have void ratios above 0.75, as for E.

[64] Stability of a High Dump

[65] Dumps 150–700 m high with an overall slope of 2H:1V on a competent, drained, level foundation were analyzed in Slide2 (Rocscience year) with the Morgenstern and Price (1965) method (half-sine interslice function) and a noncircular search optimized by simulated annealing, using the envelopes tabulated from 0.05 MPa with linear interpolation to the origin (Table 5). The section is a uniform face without benches or lifts, dry, with void ratio and unit weight constant with depth; lift-scale stability at the angle of repose is outside the scope. The unit weight was consistent with the void ratio: 17.2 kN/m³ for e = 0.65 (specific gravity 2.75, water content 5%), 15.7 kN/m³ for E (e = 0.80), and 21 kN/m³ at laboratory density. The critical surfaces ran from the toe to the crest, 31 m deep at 150 m and 137 m at 700 m, and mobilized  averaging 1.4 MPa and at most 2.0 MPa at 700 m (γ·h·cos²α at the slice bases), within the validity domain. For 700 m, the factor of safety was 1.47 with the median and 1.21 with the 5th-percentile dump-average envelope, and 1.69 and 1.60 at laboratory density and size (Table 5, which also lists the sensitivities to density, size, and envelope form). The quadratic form gave 1.20 with the 5th percentile, because at these stresses the forms agree, and a circular search (Auto Refine) gave 1.209 against 1.205. In a Monte Carlo analysis, each of 600 envelope samples was analyzed in Slide2 with its own search of the critical surface. The model correlation gave 0 failures (probability of failure below 0.6% at 95% confidence, Clopper–Pearson; Clopper and Pearson 1934; lognormal reliability index 3.3 from the median and 5th percentile of the factor of safety), and a surface confined to E gave 11 of 600 (0.9–3.3%; index 2.1). The 5th percentile of the factor of safety (1.22) agreed with the factor of safety computed with the 5th-percentile envelope (1.21), which supports deterministic analyses with percentile envelopes for this mechanism. These are strength-only probabilities, conditional on the size model, a drained foundation, and a two-dimensional section.

[66] Discussion

[67] What Leps-P Adds

[68] Leps-P does not predict the central strength of an untested material better than Leps 2.0, and with few site tests its advantage over simple fits is not statistically demonstrable with the available publications. Its contributions are a material-level envelope whose curvature decays with stress, strongly favored by the likelihood and consistent with the suppression of dilatancy; intervals consistent with nominal coverage where a constant dispersion is not, both without and with site tests; an updating rule with a conflict check; and a decomposition showing that, under the adopted size model, particle size and the identity of the material dominate the uncertainty of an untested waste rock. Large-scale tests of the actual waste rock, and of one material at two particle sizes, are therefore the most valuable investigations. The stress-decaying curvature matters only for mechanisms that reach several megapascals, such as those controlled by a weak foundation or a basal layer, which were not analyzed here.

[69] Design Format

[70] A procedure consistent with these results is: (1) assign rock quality and placement void ratio to each rock type, reduce any site tests with the maximum-deviator criterion, and check the conflict diagnostic; (2) update the level of the envelopes with the site tests (Eq. 8), jointly when one laboratory tested several materials, and exclude flagged sets only when a review of the tests identifies a cause or when exclusion is conservative; (3) shift the envelopes to placement density and field particle size; (4) adopt as characteristic value for a global mechanism the 5th percentile of the mean envelope, a Bayesian cautious estimate of the mean (Prästings et al. 2019) in the spirit of Eurocode 7 (CEN 2004, 2024); and (5) check the probability of failure by sampling the envelopes with stated correlations between rock types, including the weakest-rock-type case. The factor of safety computed with the characteristic envelope is the strength factor that the design would still tolerate, so applying mining acceptance criteria calibrated for best-estimate strength (Read and Stacey 2009; Hawley and Cunning 2017) on top of it partly counts the strength uncertainty twice. The example shows the tension: with a partial factor of 1.25 on tan φ′, as in the material-factor approach of Eurocode 7, the characteristic factor of safety of 1.21 would not satisfy the check, although the conditional probability of failure was below 0.6%. No target values are proposed here; the pairing of factor of safety and conditional probability of failure in Table 5 is an example to be calibrated site by site.

[71] Limitations

[72] The validity domain is  ≤ 8 MPa and, above 3 MPa, e ≤ 0.55, where 83 of the 90 high-stress tests lie; percentiles beyond it are model extrapolations. Tests above 3 MPa come from eight publications, the lower tail of the population is truncated at  = 31°, class labels may carry information from measured strength, and the within-material residual is laboratory scatter rather than spatial variability. The size-effect heterogeneity, the largest term at field scale, rests on 12 publications. The common asymptote is a structural assumption. The site application uses one laboratory, one tested set per rock type, a size shift shared by all rock types, and illustrative proportions. Pore pressure in saturated basal layers, wetting collapse (Oldecop and Alonso 2001), seismic loading, three-dimensional geometry, and static liquefaction are outside the scope. Leps-P can be re-estimated as drained tests at  of 5–10 MPa, tests of one material at two sizes, and in situ density data become available.

[73] Conclusions

[74] An envelope in which the dilatant part of tan  decays with stress toward an asymptote, with a material-level dilatant level and decay rate, is strongly favored by the likelihood over fixed-form alternatives; the asymptote (about 24°) is a decay parameter, not a critical-state angle.

[75] Out of sample, the bounded, quadratic, and Leps 2.0 envelopes predict new materials with indistinguishable accuracy; both hierarchical forms have intervals consistent with nominal coverage at low stress, where a constant dispersion does not, and both underpredict the median above 2.75 MPa.

[76] Updating the level of each material with a few site tests at low stress gives predictions at higher stress with coverage consistent with nominal; its advantage over simpler fits is not statistically significant with 13 publications.

[77] No systematic specimen-size effect is detected within materials; under the adopted model its heterogeneity dominates the uncertainty of an untested waste rock at field scale.

[78] For a 700 m dump on a competent foundation, whose critical surface mobilizes about 2 MPa, placement density lowers the median strength and size uncertainty the cautious strength, and the envelope form does not affect the factor of safety.
