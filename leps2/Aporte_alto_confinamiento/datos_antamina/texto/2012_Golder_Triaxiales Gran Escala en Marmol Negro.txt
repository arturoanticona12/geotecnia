1.0 
INTRODUCTION 
This Technical Memorandum presents a summary of results from the laboratory testing program carried out to 
investigate the stress-deformation behaviour and strength of the black marble rockfill that is being used to build 
Antamina Tailings Dam. The laboratory testing program was performed as part of the ongoing Dam Deformation 
Study and included specialized, large scale oedometer and triaxial tests on rockfill samples.  The rockfill samples 
were collected at Antamina as described in Report No. 379, Supervisión de Ensayos y Muestreo de Roca, and 
sent to the Instituto de Investigación y Ensayos de Materiales (IDIEM) of the University of Chile to be tested.  The 
large scale triaxial equipment at IDIEM allows the testing of 1-m diameter by 1.8-m high samples, with maximum 
particle size of 178 mm.  The data and results obtained at IDIEM during the testing program are included in 
Appendix 1. 
 
2.0 
LABORATORY TESTING PROGRAM FOR ROCKFILL 
The laboratory testing program included the following types of tests: 
 
grain size distribution (macro gradation and laboratory gradation); 
 
triaxial, Isotropically Consolidated Drained (CID) tests on 1-m diameter by 1.8-m high samples; 
 
oedometer test on 1-m diameter by 0.9-m high sample; 
 
constant head permeability tests; and 
 
post-test grain size distribution.

4.0 
ASSESSMENT OF ROCKFILL STRENGTH AND STRESS-DEFORMATION 
CHARACTERISTICS 
The strength and stress-deformation characteristics of the black marble rockfill under different stress levels and 
stress paths were investigated through triaxial compression CID and oedometer tests.  The triaxial tests included 
paths of monotonic loading and irregular cycles of loading-unloading-reloading.  The latter will be refer to here as 
unload-reload tests to emphasize that the irregular loading cycles did not include extension loading. 
Unload-reload tests were chosen as an alternative to fully cyclic tests because the current set-up of the large 
scale triaxial equipment at IDIEM does not allow extension loads to be applied. Nevertheless, important 
information regarding the rockfill cyclic behaviour was obtained through the unload-reload tests.  These data will 
serve as basis for calibrating the numerical models with which the seismic performance of Antamina Tailings 
Dam will be predicted.

The strength of individual black marble rock samples has been previously evaluated through unconfined 
compression tests on 8 cylindrical specimens (e.g. Photograph 2) as documented in Report No. 379, Supervisión

4.2.1 
Sample Reconstitution 
The rockfill specimens were prepared inside a 1-m diameter and 1.8-m high mould. Two rubber membranes (5-
mm thickness) were first attached to the mould.  The membranes were then covered with layers of non-woven 
geotextile to prevent the membranes from being punctured by the rockfill material. Rockfill material was then 
placed and compacted in 0.3-m thick layers with a small vibrating tamper (Photograph 4) to achieve a dry density 
of 2.1 t/m3.  Six layers of dry rockfill were used to construct the specimens.  Once the sample was prepared, it 
was subjected to a vacuum pressure of 0.3 kg/cm2 and the mould was removed.  The sample was finally placed 
inside the triaxial chamber using a crane (Photograph 5).

4.2.2 
Saturation of the Specimen 
After placing the specimen in the triaxial chamber, the vacuum pressure was replaced by chamber pressure and 
CO2 was circulated for 24 hours. Subsequently, deaired, distilled water was circulated through the specimen until 
the water left the sample at a constant rate. A back pressure of 2 kg/cm2 was then applied to the specimen and 
the Skempton parameter (B) was estimated.  The sample was considered saturated when a B value greater than 
95% was obtained.

4.2.4 
Deviatoric Stress Stage 
The monotonic tests were loaded in increments until an axial strain (εa) of about 20% was reached.  The loading 
schedule for the unload-reload tests consisted of three stages (Figure 2).  Stage 1 (dotted line segments) 
included monotonic loading to about 33% of the estimated ultimate shear strength (τu) followed by a full 
unload-reload cycle.  In Stage 2 (solid line segments), the load was subsequently increased to about 50% of τu 
after which the samples were subjected to three partial unload-reload cycles of increasing amplitude.  Stage 3 
(double line segments) had two variants: (i) for the test at σ’c=5 kg/cm2 (in blue), four full unload-reload cycles 
were applied at increasingly higher stress ratio levels, starting at about 50% of τu, and (ii) for the tests at σ’c =10 
and 20 kg/cm2 (in red and green), fifteen full unload-reload cycles were applied immediately after the partial 
cycles at about 50% of τu while maintaining the maximum stress ratio approximately constant.  In both cases 
Stage 3 was followed by monotonic loading to εa~20%.  The loading increments were of variable magnitude, but 
small enough to define the stress-strain curves with reasonable detail.

Results of Large Scale Triaxial Tests

The rockfill deformation modulus was estimated using triaxial test results from two stages: (i) the initial stage 
where the confining stress is isotropic (σ’1/σ’3 = 1) and (ii) the test stage in which the vertical stress is 
approximately twice the horizontal stress (σ’1/σ’3 = 2).  The latter stage was chosen because it corresponds to a 
common initial anisotropic stress state in the field and is also similar to the stress state in the oedometer test.  
The initial secant deformation modulus applicable to isotropic confining stress conditions (Ei1) was calculated 
from the first load increment (Δσd1) as Ei1 = Δσd1/Δεa1, where Δεa1 is the axial strain increment caused by Δσd1.  
The secant deformation modulus applicable to the chosen anisotropic confining stress condition (Ei2) was 
calculated as Ei2 = (σ1-σ3)/εa when (σ’1/σ’3) = 2.  The calculated values of Ei1 and Ei2 are plotted versus initial 
confining stress (σ’3c) on Figures 7a and 7b, respectively. 
The computed values of Ei1 are highly sensitive to the size of the loading increment and sample setting effects at 
the beginning of loading.  The size of the loading increment (Δσd) determines the resolution with which the stress-
strain curve is defined, which is particularly important where the slope of the curve changes most rapidly (e.g., the 
initial segment).  This is why the computed value of Ei1 from the monotonic test at σ’c=5 kg/cm2 (Curve 1) is 
smaller than that from the unload-reload test (Curve 2) at the same initial confining stress (see Figure 7c).  The 
difference in the computed values of Ei1 from the tests at σ’c=20 kg/cm2 could be attributed to sample setting 
effects, causing the initial slope of Curve 5 to be smaller than the slope after the 2nd load increment (see Figure 
7d). 
The estimated magnitude of Ei2 is relatively less sensitive to the size of the loading increment or sample setting 
effects.

4.3 
Large Scale Oedometer 
A large scale oedometer test was carried out on a 1-m diameter and 0.9-m high rockfill specimen. The rockfill 
sample was prepared in 0.3-m thick layers inside a large oedometer ring.  The layers were compacted with a 
small vibrating tamper to achieve a dry density of 2.1 t/m3.  Three layers of dry rockfill were used to construct the 
specimen.  The void ratio was estimated based on an assumed specific gravity (Gs) of 2.7. 
Once prepared, the sample was subjected to a seating pressure of 0.18 kg/cm2 and the change in sample height 
recorded until the settlement stopped.  Subsequently, the sample was flooded keeping the seating pressure 
constant and the wetting-induced settlement (0.8 mm, i.e., 0.09%) recorded. 
The rockfill specimen was loaded to a maximum load of 60 kg/cm2.  Loading was applied in 20 increments of 
2 kg/cm2 and four final increments of 5 kg/cm2 in order to carefully define the rockfill compressibility curve.  The 
load was held constant until no further deformation occurred for at least 100 minutes.  The time required for the 
deformation to stabilize after application of each loading increment became significantly longer when the total 
load applied exceeded 34 kg/cm2.  Each of the last two loading increments took about 15 days to stabilize.  The 
unloading phase was done in 12 stages.  This test lasted approximately two and a half months.  Results from the 
oedometer test are presented on Figures 8a and 8b.

As can be seen on Figure 8a, the slope of the compressibility curve is practically constant up to 40 kg/cm2, after 
which the curve breaks and continues with a flatter slope.  We consider that the change in slope of the 
compressibility curve could be related to changing the load increments from 2 to 5 kg/cm2 and/or to possibly not 
allowing enough time for the settlement to fully stabilize during the first two 5 kg/cm2 loading increments rather 
than the rockfill becoming less compressible.  The nearly constant slope of the compressibility curve indicates 
that the rockfill stiffness increases very mildly with increasing stress level within the range of stresses in the test, 
at least up to 40 kg/cm2.  This characteristic is likely related to particle breakage, which appears to occur from the 
beginning of the test and in such a way that the compressibility remains approximately constant.

ASSESSMENT OF THE PARTICLE BREAKAGE  
The particle hardness of the rockfill particles has already been investigated through Los Angeles Abrasion tests 
and documented in Report No. 379, Supervisión de Ensayos y Muestreo de Roca.  The results of these tests 
indicate Los Angeles Abrasion loss values of 44% and 41% for the two samples tested.

oedometer tests were done, and estimating Marsal’s (1967) breakage parameter Bg (i.e., the sum of the positive 
differences between percentage retained in each fraction).  The pre- and post-test gradation curves are shown on 
Figure 10 (Curves 0 through 6).  The values of the parameter Bg are included in Table 5.  These Bg values plot 
(Figure 11) outside the band of data compiled by Marsal (1973). Values of Bg greater than 50 are not common in 
the available literature.

DISCUSSION ON PARTICLE BREAKAGE AND CYCLIC COMPACTION 
The pre- and post-test gradation curves shown on Figure 10 reveal that particle breakage occurred in all the tests 
(triaxial and oedometer) performed, although not the same extent. 
The post-test gradation curves from the monotonic and unload-reload triaxial tests at σ’c=5 kg/cm2 (Curves 1 and 
2 on Figure 10) are essentially the same. This indicates that the unload-reload cycles did not cause more particle 
breakage than monotonic loading in spite of causing significantly more compaction (volume reduction).  Hence, 
the additional volumetric strain induced by cyclic loading (see Curve 2 on Figure 4) is inferred to be due to 
particle rearrangement without contribution from particle breakage. 
The post-test gradation curve from the oedometer test (Curve 6 on Figure 10) is also very similar to Curves 1 and 
2 in spite of the large difference in maximum vertical stress level (60 versus 23 kg/cm2, respectively).  This 
suggests that the restriction to lateral movement imposed by the oedometer ring yields stress states that cause 
less particle breakage than those in the triaxial test. 
The post-test gradation curve from the unload-reload test at σ’c=10 kg/cm2 (Curve 3 on Figure 10) indicates that 
more particle breakage occurred in this test than in the oedometer and both triaxial tests at σ’c=5 kg/cm2, but less 
than in the two triaxial tests at σ’c=20 kg/cm2 (Curves 4 and 5 on Figure 10).  The potential effect of cyclic loading 
on particle breakage in this case cannot be assessed by simple inspection of the single post-test gradation curve.  
The additional compaction induced by the series of unload-reload cycles applied at about 50% of τu (Curve 4 on 
Figure 4) without exceeding the maximum stress level previously achieved (Curve 4 on Figure 3) is about 45% of 
the compaction induced by previous loading up to such shear stress level.  
The post-test gradation Curves 4 and 5 on Figure 10 suggest that a slightly larger extent of particle breakage 
occurred in the unload-reload test at σ’c=20 kg/cm2 than in the corresponding monotonic test.  However, the 
unload-reload cycles applied at about 50% of τu and without exceeding the maximum shear stress level (Curve 5 
on Figure 3) increased the compaction by about 80% (see Curve 5 on Figure 4) compared to that induced by 
previous loading.  Given the small difference in particle breakage between the monotonic and unload-reload 
tests, the significant additional compaction induced by cyclic loading at this stress level seems to have been 
caused by particle rearrangement with little or no contribution from particle breakage.  Furthermore, form Curves 
3 and 5 on Figure 4, the volumetric strain induced exclusively by cyclic deviatoric loading Stages 2 and 3 (see 
Figure 2) is about 45% greater in the test at σ’c=20 kg/cm2 than in the test at σ’c=10 kg/cm2.  Since the number of 
unload-reload cycles is the same in both tests, the larger amount of compaction due to cyclic loading in the test at 
σ’c=20 kg/cm2 is likely related to the amplitude of the unload-reload cycles.
